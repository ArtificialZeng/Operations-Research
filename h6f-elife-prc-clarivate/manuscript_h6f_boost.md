## Abstract

Evaluating publish–review–curate (PRC) journals requires linking each manuscript's initial reviewed preprint ($v_1$) to its subsequent public revisions, formal Version of Record (VoR), multi-DOI citation footprint, and onsite readership without conditioning on post-$v_1$ outcomes or leaking post-revision covariates. Across the complete census of 4,551 *eLife* PRC manuscripts (4,541 recovered $v_1$ assessments, 99.78%; $N = 2,653$ mature 2023–2024 cohorts including 2,315 VoRs and 338 non-VoR reviewed preprints), we conduct a three-stage scientometric audit. First, we document two critical measurement hazards in versioned publishing: (i) look-ahead covariate leakage, where taking maximum author counts across versions exceeds $v_1$ authorship in 368/2,285 (16.1%) benchmark VoRs (shifting the $v_1$ below-*Solid* citation association from -14.03% to -13.74% [-18.97%, -8.18%]) and final primary subjects differ from $v_1$ subjects in 441/4,551 articles; and (ii) asymmetric OpenAlex multi-DOI canonicalization, where a stratified citing-work ID deduplication audit ($N = 200$, mean 4.53 DOIs/article) shows 98.3% single-work consolidation among VoRs versus 95.0% multi-work splitting between bioRxiv/medRxiv and *eLife* records among non-VoR preprints (yearly-max proxy vs. exact citing-ID union: eLife-only $r = 0.99979$, all-DOI $r = 0.99672$, aggregate ratio 0.9716). Second, freezing all covariates strictly at $v_1$ publication, initial below-*Solid* evidence assessments strongly predict lower subsequent citations over equal next-full-calendar-year windows across all mature $v_1$ preprints (-22.6% [-29.1%, -15.4%], $p = 1.17e-05$, adding +3.09 pp Poisson pseudo-$R^2$), within VoRs (-18.2% [-23.6%, -12.4%]), and within non-VoR reviewed preprints (-30.1% [-51.1%, -0.1%] post-$v_1$, $p = 0.049$), while switching solely from $v_1$-quarter to VoR-quarter fixed effects attenuates VoR estimates (-12.9% to -9.5%). Third, separating denominators across 964 mature $v_1$ below-*Solid* preprints shows 842/964 (87.3%) posted public revisions, 793/964 (82.3%) reached VoR, 644/964 (66.8% unconditional; 644/793 = 81.2% VoR-conditional) upgraded evidence ratings over a mean 325.4-day $v_1$-to-VoR calendar interval, and 192/216 (88.9%) final below-*Solid* VoRs posted public revisions. Observational contrasts between upgraded and non-upgraded VoRs are imprecise (+3.2% [-10.7%, +19.2%] benchmark; +8.6% [-9.3%, +30.0%] strict $v_1$ covariates) and neither establish statistical equivalence to null effects nor isolate causal reader badge reactions amid *eLife*'s June 2024 discoverability redesign.

**Keywords:** Publish–Review–Curate; *eLife* Assessments; Open Peer Review; Version Linking; OpenAlex Deduplication; Predictive Validity; Scientometrics

## 1. Introduction

Since January 2023, *eLife* has operated a Publish–Review–Curate (PRC) publishing model that eliminates traditional binary accept/reject decisions after peer review (Stern & O'Shea, 2019; Eisen et al., 2020, 2022; Avissar-Whiting et al., 2024). Manuscripts selected for peer review are published as citable Reviewed Preprints accompanied by public peer reviews and a structured *eLife* Assessment rating two dimensions on controlled vocabularies: *Significance of Findings* (*Useful*, *Valuable*, *Important*, *Fundamental*, *Landmark*) and *Strength of Evidence* (*Inadequate*, *Incomplete*, *Solid*, *Convincing*, *Compelling*, *Exceptional*) (Hardwicke et al., 2024; Behrens et al., 2024, 2026). Authors retain full control over whether to post public revised preprints, leave assessments unchanged, or declare a formal Version of Record (VoR).

For scientometrics, this architecture creates both a rare prospective laboratory and an acute measurement challenge. Unlike traditional journals where only surviving accepted manuscripts enter bibliographic databases—masking reviewer disagreement and revision dynamics (Tomkins et al., 2017; Teplitskiy et al., 2018; Card & DellaVigna, 2020)—PRC exposes initial ($v_1$) peer evaluations, public version histories, and downstream citation and usage outcomes in machine-readable form. Yet precisely because PRC articles evolve through multiple citable manifestations (an upstream bioRxiv or medRxiv preprint, $v_1$ through $v_k$ *eLife* Reviewed Preprints, and an optional VoR), standard bibliometric workflows introduce three systematic distortions if applied naively:

1. **Final-state and date-clock conflation.** Retrieving metadata from the latest article endpoint or indexing cohorts by VoR publication date silently substitutes post-revision assessments, post-revision author lists, post-revision subject tags, and post-revision dates for the information actually observable at $v_1$. Because lower-rated manuscripts revise longer before finalizing (mean 325.4 calendar days from $v_1$ to VoR), aligning cohorts on VoR dates pairs older, revised papers against younger, initially stronger papers, confounding both timing and quality.
2. **Multi-DOI citation fragmentation and double counting.** A single PRC manuscript routinely carries four to six registered DOIs across preprint servers and *eLife* versions (Fu & Hughey, 2019; Abdill & Blekhman, 2019; Fraser et al., 2020). Whether bibliographic aggregators such as OpenAlex merge those manifestations into one canonical work record—and whether that merging differs systematically between finalized VoRs and non-VoR reviewed preprints—directly governs citation measurement error.
3. **Denominator mixing and causal over-interpretation.** Conditioning upgrade rates exclusively on finalized VoRs conflates revision propensity with VoR selection; labeling final below-*Solid* VoRs as "unrevised" ignores authors who posted public revisions without securing a rating upgrade; and interpreting observational article-level attention differences as causal reader reactions to rating badges ignores both unobserved manuscript quality and concurrent platform interface changes. In particular, *eLife* confirmed that starting in June 2024 it rolled out phased discoverability and interface changes—moving *eLife* Assessments above the abstract, adding a vocabulary dropdown, displaying color-coded article stage badges in search results, and enabling readers to browse by minimum *Significance* and *Strength of Evidence* thresholds (eLife, 2025).

Two landmark empirical studies establish the immediate benchmark for our work. First, Mutz et al. (2025) compared the reliability of *eLife*'s legacy and new peer-review processes in *Scientometrics*, evaluating rating agreement and vocabulary properties without tracking version-linked longitudinal citation windows, multi-DOI OpenAlex deduplication error, or article-level readership. Second, *eLife* reported an internal analysis of assessment shifts across 2,051 finalized VoRs (Adamson, 2026), explicitly excluding 351 articles with multi-term strength assessments, excluding all non-VoR reviewed preprints, and reporting no citation or onsite readership outcomes. Our incremental contribution to *Scientometrics* is a **prospective, reproducible, version-level measurement and predictive-validity framework** spanning the entire public PRC census (4,551 manuscripts; 4,541 recovered $v_1$ assessments, including multi-term assessments and non-VoR preprints) that: (a) audits look-ahead covariate leakage and OpenAlex citing-work ID deduplication across multi-DOI records; (b) tests the incremental predictive value of strictly $v_1$-frozen assessments over equal-length citation windows across all $v_1$ preprints, VoRs, and non-VoR preprints separately; and (c) cleanly decomposes public revisions, VoR conversion, unconditional versus VoR-conditional rating upgrades, and onsite readership trajectories with transparent precision and equivalence bounds.

Throughout this study, we adhere to a strict predictive-association estimand. Because article-level observational aggregates cannot separate public badge visibility from underlying manuscript quality, author self-selection into revision, or the phased June 2024 interface redesign, we make **no causal claims** about reader responses to displayed rating badges. Similarly, because public data lack internal submission, desk-review, and transfer logs—and because *eLife*'s December 10, 2024 indexing announcement simultaneously modified coverage in both Web of Science and Scopus (eLife, 2024a–c)—all exploratory cohort, manuscript-ID velocity, synthetic-control, and country-share analyses around the indexing transition are separated cleanly into our companion Supplementary Information (`supplementary_h6f_scientometrics.pdf`) rather than presented as causal policy effects in the main text.

## 2. Data Architecture, Version Linking, Covariate Freezing, and Multi-DOI Deduplication Audit

### 2.1 Public Census, Version Linking, and Telemetry Architecture

We assembled a frozen public-data snapshot anchored on November 25, 2026 (`snapshot_date = 2026-11-25`) linking four open scholarly infrastructure endpoints:

1. ***eLife* PRC Search Census & Immutable $v_1$ Assessment Snapshots.** The public *eLife* search API yielded 4,551 unique PRC manuscripts posted from 2023 through November 2026. Querying each manuscript's dedicated version-1 record (`version=1`) recovered machine-readable $v_1$ *Strength of Evidence* ratings for 4,541/4,551 (99.78%) articles, with 0 missing $v_1$ publication dates and 0 chronological anomalies (VoR date preceding $v_1$ date) across all 3,264 VoRs. Following *eLife*'s vocabulary hierarchy, multi-term ratings are conservatively indexed by their minimum strength tier (`v1_str_min`), pooling *Inadequate* and *Incomplete* as below *Solid* (`v1_str_min <= 2`) against the *Solid* (`= 3`) baseline, alongside *Convincing* (`= 4`) and *Compelling/Exceptional* (`>= 5`). To guarantee at least one complete post-$v_1$ calendar year of citation follow-up, our primary predictive-validity panel comprises all mature 2023–2024 $v_1$ cohorts with non-missing $v_1$ strength assessments ($N = 2,653$, consisting of 2,315 finalized VoRs and 338 non-VoR reviewed preprints).
2. **Crossref Versioned DOI Histories & Public Revision Identification.** We linked all 20,211 Crossref DOI records (`10.7554/eLife.*`, spanning both `posted-content` reviewed-preprint versions $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_k$ and `journal-article` VoR records) to the 4,551-paper census. Crucially, we define a **publicly posted revision** independently of any change in the *eLife* Assessment: an article is coded as having a posted revision if and only if Crossref records at least one distinct `posted-content` DOI with `ver >= 2` dated strictly after $v_1$, on or before November 25, 2026, and (for VoRs) on or before the VoR publication date. This measures observable public releases; edits performed privately before VoR declaration without an intermediate revised preprint remain unobserved.
3. **OpenAlex Multi-DOI Citation Histories.** We matched every *eLife* DOI variant and linked upstream bioRxiv/medRxiv preprint DOI (`v1_preprint_doi`) against OpenAlex. To avoid mechanical double-counting across version DOIs of the same manuscript while preserving calendar-year granularity, our full-panel citation endpoints compute the maximum citation count per calendar year across DOI variants, evaluated over two strictly post-$v_1$ windows: (i) all post-$v_1$ calendar years (`year > v1_year`, excluding the posting year), and (ii) a strictly equal-length **next full calendar year** (`year == v1_year + 1`, i.e., 2024 for 2023 $v_1$ cohorts and 2025 for 2024 $v_1$ cohorts). We compute both eLife-DOI-family and combined eLife-plus-preprint versions of each endpoint and audit this yearly-max convention directly against exact citing-work ID deduplication in Section 2.3.
4. **Live *eLife* Onsite Readership Telemetry & Anti-Truncation Guard.** We harvested lifetime (`totalValue`) and monthly (`by=month`) article-level `page-views` and `downloads` from the public *eLife* Metrics API, achieving 2,653/2,653 (100.00%) lifetime coverage on mature 2023–2024 cohorts. Auditing the API payload revealed a strict 37-month rolling window (`2023-11` through `2026-11`, with `2026-10` as the last complete month). Articles posted before November 2023 (801 mature articles) are left-truncated in monthly arrays and are strictly assigned `NaN` rather than silently imputed as zero. All monthly readership models restrict cleanly to the untruncated $v_1 \ge$ `2023-11` cohort ($N = 1,852$), controlling explicitly for `v1_posting_day` in first-three-calendar-bin endpoints to absorb partial launch-month exposure. All onsite metrics aggregate traffic across available versions of an article and overlap *eLife*'s phased June 2024 discoverability redesign (eLife, 2025).

### 2.2 Look-Ahead Covariate Leakage Audit: Freezing Strictly at-$v_1$ Information

A foundational requirement of prospective predictive-validity modeling is that every conditioning covariate must be strictly measurable at $v_1$ publication, before subsequent revisions, author additions, or subject reclassifications occur. Auditing earlier pipeline versions uncovered two distinct sources of look-ahead covariate leakage:

- **Post-revision author-count leakage.** Aggregating Crossref author counts via `max(n_authors)` across all versions of a manuscript leaks co-authors added during revision (for instance, new collaborators recruited to run validation experiments). Across the complete census, `cr_n_authors_max > v1_n_authors` in 554/4,551 (12.2%) manuscripts (399/2,653 mature manuscripts). On the exact fixed VoR benchmark sample ($N = 2,285$), exactly **368/2,285 (16.1%)** articles had maximum author counts exceeding their true $v_1$ Crossref `posted-content` (`ver == 1`) author count (`0` missing across all 4,551 manuscripts). Moreover, among $v_1$ below-*Solid* VoRs, manuscripts that later upgraded to *Solid+* added co-authors more frequently than those remaining below *Solid* (21.7% vs. 15.1%), meaning `max_authors` conditioned on a post-$v_1$ revision mediator.
- **Post-revision subject-classification drift.** Comparing `v1_subjects[0]` from the $v_1$ API payload against the final search-census `subjects[0]` revealed discrepancies in **441/4,551 (9.7%)** manuscripts (301 reordered within an identical multi-subject set and 140 involving added or replaced discipline tags; 309/2,653 mature articles, and 302/2,284 in the VoR benchmark).

In all primary `v4` specifications below, we eliminate both leakage channels completely by freezing **strictly at-$v_1$ covariates**: `v1_n_authors` (from Crossref `posted-content ver == 1`), `v1_primary_subject` (from `v1_subjects[0]`), `v1_quarter` (plus `v1_month` robustness), `v1_age_days`, `v1_posting_day`, and strictly pre-$v_1$ citations (`year < v1_year`, verified against strictly preprint-DOI pre-$v_1$ citations). We also retain the benchmark specification side-by-side to document exact step-by-step parity.

### 2.3 OpenAlex Citing-Work ID Deduplication Audit on Multi-DOI Works

For every audited article, we resolved all registered Crossref *eLife* version DOIs plus the linked bioRxiv/medRxiv DOI against OpenAlex, fetched the complete set of unique citing OpenAlex Work IDs and publication years for every resolved work record, and compared three aggregation rules on the exact same OpenAlex timestamp: (i) the **exact deduplicated citing-work ID union**, (ii) our **yearly-max proxy**, and (iii) **naive summing** across OpenAlex records.

Table 1 and Figure 1 uncover a sharp, previously undocumented **asymmetric canonicalization pattern in OpenAlex** with direct consequences for PRC bibliometrics:

1. **VoRs consolidate almost completely; non-VoR reviewed preprints remain split.** Across the 120 audited VoRs, **118/120 (98.3%)** collapsed into a **single** canonical OpenAlex Work ID merging the bioRxiv/medRxiv preprint with all *eLife* versions (only 2/120 remained split; aggregate `ymax/union` = 1.0000 for $v_1 <$ *Solid* and 0.9977 for *Solid+*). By stark contrast, across the 80 audited non-VoR reviewed preprints, **76/80 (95.0%)** remained split across two distinct OpenAlex Work IDs—one for the upstream preprint server and one for the *eLife* Reviewed Preprint (while only 2/200 [1.0%] ever split across multiple *eLife*-prefixed Work IDs).
2. **Yearly-max is near-exact for *eLife* DOIs and mildly conservative across split preprint/RP pairs.** For *eLife*-prefixed DOIs alone, yearly-max matches the exact citing-work ID union on **99.0%** of audited manuscripts (Pearson $r = 0.99979$, Spearman $\rho = 0.99930$, median ratio 1.0000, aggregate ratio 0.9980), and matches our frozen snapshot closely (frozen vs. live *eLife* union $r = 0.99913$, mean 9.75 vs. 9.76). Across all manifestations including unmerged preprints, yearly-max achieves $r = 0.99672$, $\rho = 0.99560$, median ratio 1.0000, and aggregate ratio 0.9716 (a mild 2.84% undercount overall, or 12.77% within the 78 split works; next-full-year $r = 0.99506$, aggregate ratio 0.9704). Conversely, naive summing inflates split-work citations by +12.34% (aggregate sum/union = 1.1234) due to 63 citing papers across 29 manuscripts citing both manifestations simultaneously.
3. **Minimal regression distortion.** Because non-VoR undercount ratios are nearly symmetric between below-*Solid* (0.8774) and *Solid+* (0.8725), PPML estimates of the $v_1$ below-*Solid* association on the audited sample ($n = 194$) are virtually unchanged between exact citing-ID unions and yearly-max proxies: -25.43% vs. -25.38% (*eLife*-only; frozen snapshot -25.19%), and -27.28% vs. -26.21% (all manifestations, post-$v_1$; next-year -29.09% vs. -27.73%).

### 2.4 Econometric Specification and Statistical Inference

For non-negative skewed citation and usage count endpoints, we estimate fixed-effects Poisson Pseudo-Maximum Likelihood (PPML) models, which remain consistent under arbitrary overdispersion and avoid the scale-dependent distortions of `log(1 + y)` transformations when zeros are present (Santos Silva & Tenreyro, 2006; Chen & Roth, 2024):

$$E[Y_i \mid X_i, \alpha_{s(i), t(i)}] = \exp\!\left(\beta_1 \mathbf{1}[\text{BelowSolid}_i] + \beta_2 \mathbf{1}[\text{Conv}_i] + \beta_3 \mathbf{1}[\text{Compel+}_i] + \gamma_1 \mathbf{1}[\text{Imp}_i] + \gamma_2 \mathbf{1}[\text{Fund+}_i] + \delta^\top Z_i^{(v_1)} + \alpha_{s(i), t(i)}\right)$$

where $\alpha_{s(i), t(i)}$ denotes strictly $v_1$-frozen `v1_primary_subject × v1_quarter` fixed effects (with `v1_primary_subject × v1_month` robustness checks), $Z_i^{(v_1)}$ contains strictly frozen $v_1$ covariates (`v1_age_days`, `log(1 + v1_n_authors)`, `log(1 + pre_v1_cites)`, plus `v1_posting_day` for first-three-bin usage), and all standard errors and 95% confidence intervals are CR1 cluster-robust at the discipline level ($G = 17$–$18$, using $t_{G-1}$ critical values). Reported percentage associations equal $100 \times (\exp(\hat{\beta}) - 1)$. For within-article log-change readership trajectories and event studies, we estimate within-FE OLS with CR1 standard errors and restricted Rademacher wild cluster bootstrap $p$-values ($B = 1{,}999$; Cameron, Gelbach, & Miller, 2008). To quantify whether observational upgrade contrasts rule out substantively meaningful effects rather than misinterpreting statistical non-significance as "zero return," we report exact multiplicative Two One-Sided Tests (TOST; Lakens, 2017) against pre-specified relative margins of $\pm 15\%$ ($\ln(0.85)$ to $\ln(1.15)$) and $\pm 20\%$ ($\ln(0.80)$ to $\ln(1.20)$).

1. **Covariate freezing modestly refines—and never eliminates—the $v_1$ gradient.** Replacing leaked `max_authors` with strictly frozen `v1_n_authors` on the exact $N = 2,285$ benchmark shifts the post-$v_1$ citation association by 0.29 percentage points, from -14.03% to **-13.74% [-18.97%, -8.18%]** ($p = 0.0001$). Table 6 and Figure 4 deliver two vital corrective lessons for evaluating public revision outcomes:

1. **Precision limits preclude claiming "zero return" to upgrading.** Under the earlier benchmark covariates (`max_authors` + final subject FE), upgrading to *Solid+* carries a post-$v_1$ OpenAlex citation point estimate of **+3.2% [-10.7%, +19.2%]** ($p = 0.654$; TOST $\pm 20\%$ $p = 0.021$, $\pm 15\%$ $p = 0.066$). Once look-ahead author and subject leakage are removed (freezing both `v1_n_authors` and `v1_primary_subject`), the observational point estimate rises to **+8.6% [-9.3%, +30.0%]** ($p = 0.348$; next full calendar year **+5.9% [-14.6%, +31.4%]**, $p = 0.580$; Crossref **+14.5% [-3.8%, +36.3%]**, $p = 0.119$), and **neither** $\pm 15\%$ (TOST $p = 0.253$) **nor** $\pm 20\%$ (TOST $p = 0.127$) equivalence is established. The downward shift under leaked covariates is partly consistent with added co-authors acting as a post-$v_1$ mediator of revision effort (21.7% of upgraded VoRs added co-authors vs. 15.1% of non-upgraded VoRs). Thus, current sample sizes ($n = 765$, with only 172 non-upgraded comparators) are compatible with anything from modest negative differences ($-9\%$) to substantial positive associations ($+25\%$ to $+30\%$); describing this wide confidence interval as "zero return" would be statistically invalid.
2. **Onsite traffic co-moves strongly with version-release events rather than differing sharply by rating outcome.** In the clean untruncated local VoR event study ($N = 651$ raw; $n = 614$ in FE), VoR publication triggers a virtually identical month-$0$ surge over months $-3..-1$ regardless of whether the rating upgraded to *Solid+* or remained below *Solid* (raw mean log-view jump +1.11 vs. +1.11; downloads +1.80 vs. +1.76; FE contrast +0.061 log [-0.042, +0.164], $p_{\text{WCB}} = 0.273$). Post-release months $+1..+6$ likewise show imprecise, near-zero contrasts for views (-0.062 log [-0.198, +0.073], $p_{\text{WCB}} = 0.369$) and downloads (+0.026 log [-0.135, +0.186], $p_{\text{WCB}} = 0.736$). By contrast, in balanced 22-month post-$v_1$ trajectories (`months 12–21` vs. `0–2`), each additional publicly posted version (`cr_n_versions`) is strongly associated with sustained page-view activity in both $v_1$ below-*Solid* VoRs (+0.248 log [+0.081, +0.415], $p_{\text{WCB}} = 0.019$) and $v_1$ *Solid* VoRs (+0.333 log [+0.188, +0.478], $p_{\text{WCB}} = 0.005$), while VoR conversion itself correlates with substantially higher cumulative attention across all 964 below-*Solid* preprints (views +0.589 log [+0.499, +0.678], downloads +1.012 log [+0.899, +1.126], both $p_{\text{WCB}} = 0.0005$). Because release timing and revision decisions are jointly chosen by authors, these patterns capture observational release-event dynamics rather than isolated badge effects.

### 4.3 Methodological Scope Boundary: Indexing-Announcement Analyses Deferred to Supplementary Information

On October 23–24, 2024, Clarivate placed *eLife* on hold in Web of Science; on November 20, 2024, *eLife* described a proposed selective feed restricted to *Solid+* VoRs; and on December 10, 2024, *eLife* announced **simultaneous indexing changes across both Web of Science and Scopus**—supplying a partial *Solid+* VoR feed to Web of Science (forgoing Journal Impact Factor eligibility) while transitioning from the Scopus Journals Collection to the Scopus Preprints Collection (eLife, 2024a–c). Because public metadata lack internal submission, desk-review, and transfer timestamps across comparable journals—and because **166/514 (32.3%)** of earlier-cohort ($v_1 <$ October 1, 2024) 450-day VoRs actually finalized *after* the December 10 announcement—before-after posting-cohort contrasts cannot isolate a causal indexing-policy effect. Indeed, as documented fully in Supplementary Information (`supplementary_h6f_scientometrics.pdf`, Section S2), once follow-up is held strictly equal at 450 days with subject adjustment, differences between post-December 10 and pre-October 1 posting cohorts are statistically indistinguishable from zero for both final below-*Solid* VoRs (-2.76 pp [-7.54, +2.02], $p_{\text{WCB}} = 0.195$) and posted public revisions (-3.47 pp [-10.84, +3.90], $p_{\text{WCB}} = 0.334$). All exploratory indexing-cohort survival curves, sequential manuscript-ID velocity proxies, published-output synthetic-control series, and geographic tabulations are therefore reported transparently in the Supplementary Information rather than claimed as causal findings here.

## 5. Discussion, Limitations, and Roadmaps for Causal Follow-Up

### 5.1 Incremental Scientometric Contribution

Relative to Mutz et al. (2025), who evaluated the reliability of *eLife*'s review process, and Adamson (2026), who summarized single-term rating shifts among 2,051 finalized VoRs, this study demonstrates why **prospective version-level measurement design** is decisive when evaluating PRC publishing. Three practical lessons emerge for scientometricians, research evaluators, and open-review publishers:

1. **Preserve immutable $v_1$ metadata snapshots.** Reconstructing $v_1$ author counts (`v1_n_authors`), $v_1$ primary discipline tags (`v1_primary_subject`), and $v_1$ posting dates eliminates look-ahead leakage affecting 368/2,285 (16.1%) VoRs on authorship and 441/4,551 (9.7%) manuscripts on primary discipline—leakage that substantively shifts post-review upgrade comparisons by conditioning on revision-stage co-author additions.
2. **Account for asymmetric preprint-to-VoR merging in bibliographic databases.** OpenAlex merges upstream bioRxiv/medRxiv preprints into canonical *eLife* works for 98.3% of VoRs but only 5.0% of non-VoR reviewed preprints. Consequently, *eLife*-DOI-only citation queries systematically under-measure non-VoR preprints, naive cross-DOI summing double-counts overlapping citations by +12.3% on split records, and citing-work ID union deduplication should be adopted whenever PRC platforms are benchmarked against traditional journals.
3. **Evaluate prospective predictive validity on unselected $v_1$ cohorts and separate revision from re-rating.** Initial below-*Solid* assessments carry consistent incremental predictive value over equal next-full-calendar-year windows across all $v_1$ preprints (-22.6%, +3.09 pp pseudo-$R^2$), VoRs (-18.2%), and non-VoR reviewed preprints (-30.1% post-$v_1$). At the same time, 192/216 (88.9%) final below-*Solid* VoRs posted public revisions, confirming that assessment stability does not imply author inaction.

### 5.2 Limitations

Four limitations bound our conclusions. First, all citation and readership regressions are strictly **observational predictive associations**: *eLife* Assessments reflect reviewers' judgments of underlying manuscript rigor and novelty (Azoulay, Stuart, & Wang, 2014), so lower citations for below-*Solid* preprints cannot be decomposed into intrinsic quality differences versus reader reactions to public labels. Second, *eLife*'s phased June 2024 discoverability redesign concurrently altered assessment placement, search badges, and browse filters (eLife, 2025), precluding causal attribution of onsite traffic patterns without session-level deployment logs. Third, our upgrade-to-*Solid+* comparison conditions on VoR conversion and remains statistically imprecise (+8.6% [-9.3%, +30.0%] under strict $v_1$ covariates), leaving both modest positive returns and null differences empirically plausible. Fourth, public Crossref records capture posted preprint versions (`ver >= 2`) but cannot observe private editorial correspondence or manuscripts withdrawn before peer review.

### 5.3 Experimental and Administrative-Data Roadmaps for Causal Follow-Up

Moving from prospective predictive validity (the scope of *Scientometrics*) to causal identification of reader behavior or indexing-policy incentives requires two distinct empirical designs beyond public observational snapshots:

1. **Causal reader-behavior identification (path to *PLOS Biology* or *Nature Human Behaviour*).** Isolating the direct behavioral effect of public peer-review badges requires a pre-registered, platform-partnered **within-article, same-version randomized display experiment**. Specifically, incoming reader sessions arriving at the *exact same article version* through the *exact same referral entry* (e.g., PubMed, Google Scholar, or direct DOI) should be randomly assigned to high-prominence assessment placement (above abstract, expanded vocabulary) versus low-prominence control placement (collapsed or below abstract), recording session-level exposures, full-text opens, and PDF downloads over a fixed short window (primary endpoint: full-text open/download rate per exposure). Stratifying treatments across *Significance* versus *Strength of Evidence* phrasing separates headline salience from methodological caution. Where only phased UI deployment logs are obtainable, analysts must verify parallel pre-trends prior to rollout and test unchanged referral entry points as placebos. To meet the cross-context generalizability bar of *Nature Human Behaviour*, the same within-version display randomization should be pre-registered and replicated on a second open peer-review platform or independent reader panel to establish that reader responses transcend a single journal page layout.
2. **Causal indexing-policy identification (path to *Research Policy*).** Identifying how indexing rules reshape author submission, revision, and journal-substitution behavior requires confidential administrative logs linking manuscript submission, desk-review, public-revision, transfer, and VoR-declaration timestamps across *eLife* and comparable life-science journals. Exposure should be partitioned across the October 23–24, 2024 Web of Science hold, the November 20 selective-feed proposal, the December 10 dual Web of Science/Scopus announcement, and actual database ingestion dates, leveraging pre-existing institutional evaluation regimes (statutory WoS/JIF-dependent vs. DORA-reformed institutions) with common-trend and placebo tests among low-WoS-dependence groups.
